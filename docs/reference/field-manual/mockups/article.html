Turn your inbox into a weekly customer brief

A twenty-minute setup that reads a week of customer email for you and hands back the five things worth acting on.

HBy Hector Luis Alamo

Every small-business owner I know has the same weekly problem. The inbox holds a week of customers telling you, in their own words, what is working and what is not. Nobody has time to read it as a body of evidence, so it gets read as a stream of fires.

An AI assistant is good at exactly this chore: reading a lot of messy text and reporting patterns. It is bad at knowing what your business can afford to change. So the workflow below splits the job. The assistant reads. You decide.

What you need

Three things, none of them new: a mailbox with customer messages in it, an AI assistant you already pay for, and fifteen minutes one afternoon. You do not need an automation platform, a developer or a new subscription.

The one rule before you start

Decide what you are willing to paste. A customer's first name and their complaint are fine. Payment details, medical notes and anything under a confidentiality clause are not. Strip them first.

The workflow

Run it once by hand before you try to automate anything. The first brief will be mediocre, and that is useful: it tells you which question to sharpen.

  1. Collect the week. Search your mailbox for messages from customers, last seven days, and export them as plain text. One file, no attachments.
  2. Clean it. Delete signatures, legal footers and anything on the do-not-paste list. A quick word count tells you whether it will fit in one pass.
  3. Ask for patterns, not summaries. A summary repeats the email. A pattern tells you that six people asked about delivery windows.
  4. Demand evidence. Ask for a short quote behind every pattern so you can check the assistant did not invent a trend.
  5. Write the decision. Under each pattern, add one line: what you will do about it, or why you will not.
# step 2: how big is this week's file?
$ cat customers-week-44.txt | wc -w
4212
$ grep -c "refund" customers-week-44.txt
9

If you cannot say what you would do differently after reading it, the brief is not finished.

The prompt that does the work

Paste the cleaned file under a short instruction. Plain words beat clever ones. Mine reads: "Read these customer emails. List up to five patterns, ranked by how many customers mention them. For each, give two short quotes and say what is not clear from the evidence." That last sentence matters. It gives the assistant permission to say it does not know.

Check before you act

Open two of the quotes in your mailbox and confirm they exist. If either is missing, throw the brief away and run it again with a shorter file. Assistants drift on long inputs, and a made-up quote is the clearest warning you will get.

What to expect

The first few weeks, the brief will mostly confirm what you suspected. That is fine. The value shows up in week six, when a pattern appears that you had not noticed because it was spread across eleven different people on eleven different days.

Keep the briefs in one folder. After a month, ask the assistant to compare four of them and tell you what changed. That comparison is the closest thing a small shop has to a customer research department.
